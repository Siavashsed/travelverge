## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What happens to travellers if their tour operator or travel company goes bust while they're abroad?

It depends almost entirely on how you booked, which is something I've learned from years of reviewing resorts and spas and speaking with guests who never looked at the paperwork behind their trip.

The clearest recent example is Thomas Cook, which stopped trading at around 2 a.m. on 23 September 2019. In the UK, the Civil Aviation Authority organised the largest peacetime repatriation in British history, flying roughly 150,000 people home. That was possible because of ATOL, a financial protection scheme covering flight-inclusive packages sold by UK operators.

Here is how the main scenarios usually play out:

1. Protected package holiday (flight plus hotel). If the operator is ATOL-protected, you are generally flown home on your scheduled date, and your hotel stay is usually honoured or arranged through the scheme. You may also be able to claim for trips you had booked but not yet taken.

2. Flight-only or separately booked components. Protection is patchier. Flights booked alone may not be covered by ATOL, and hotels paid for directly may require you to settle up locally.

3. Paying by credit card. In the UK, Section 75 can give you a route to recover money from the card provider for purchases over a certain value, which is a useful backstop.

4. Travel insurance. Check whether it includes scheduled airline failure or supplier failure cover, as many standard policies exclude it.

My own practical checklist: look for the ATOL number on your confirmation, keep your booking references and receipts, pay by credit card where you can, and read the insurance wording before you go. Hotels, in my experience, are rarely the problem; the chain of payments behind them is.
