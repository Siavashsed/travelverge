**Title:** Paying extra for a ryokan room with a private onsen? Ask whether the water is actually from the source first

I stayed at a well-known Hakone ryokan in March 2024 and paid ¥286,000 for two people for one night. The kaiseki was unforgettable, 13 courses I can still list in order. But the thing I was really paying for, the cypress tub on our private terrace, turned out to run on reheated water, not water flowing straight from the spring.

Nothing on the booking page said so. I only found out after the stay.

Now I ask every inn the same question before I book. Is the in-room bath gensen kakenagashi, meaning free-flowing from the source? Or is it heated and recirculated? Some rooms in the ¥120,000 to ¥180,000 range say yes to the first. Some of the most expensive rooms say no.

What I took from it: the room rate follows the view, the square footage and the dinner. It does not reliably follow the water. If the bath is the reason you're splurging, get the answer in writing before you pay.

Has anyone else run into this? I'd like to know which inns have been upfront about it.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Is it worth paying for a ryokan room with a private onsen and kaiseki dinner in Japan?

It can be, but often not for the reason people expect, and the most expensive option is not always the best one.

At Japan's top ryokan, a room with its own open-air bath and a full kaiseki dinner usually costs a couple ¥120,000 to ¥400,000 a night, roughly $800 to $2,700. That is a wide range, and the price doesn't follow the thing most guests care about, which is the bath itself.

I learned this firsthand. In March 2024 I paid ¥286,000 for two people at a celebrated inn in Hakone. The 13-course kaiseki was one of the best meals I have eaten anywhere. The private cypress tub on our terrace, the reason I had booked that room, ran on reheated water instead of water flowing straight from the spring. The listing never said so.

Here is what I now do before booking any ryokan with a private bath:

1. Ask directly whether the in-room bath is gensen kakenagashi, meaning water that flows continuously from the source, or whether it is heated and recirculated. Get the answer in writing.

2. Separate what you're paying for. Higher rates usually buy a better view, a bigger room and a more elaborate dinner. Decide which of those matter to you.

3. Look seriously at the lower end of the range. Some rooms around ¥120,000 to ¥180,000 have true source-fed baths, and in my experience they are often the better stay.

4. Consider a reservable private bath (kashikiri) instead of an in-room tub if the inn's source water feeds the shared baths but not the rooms.

I wrote up the full cost breakdown from that stay here, if it helps: https://siavashsed.github.io/travelverge/japan-ryokan-private-onsen-kaiseki-cost/

In short, a kaiseki dinner is almost always worth it. The private onsen is only worth paying extra for once you know what water is in it.
