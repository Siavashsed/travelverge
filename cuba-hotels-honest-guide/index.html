Cuba Built a 42-Story Luxury Hotel Almost Nobody Sleeps In

Cuba's state hotels sit near empty while private stays thrive. A firsthand guide to where your money actually buys comfort in Havana and beyond.

The best hotel rooms in Cuba right now are mostly not in hotels, and the sooner you accept that, the better your trip gets. I checked into the Gran Hotel Manzana Kempinski in Havana in March 2024, paid about 380 dollars for a fifth-floor room, and shared the rooftop pool with exactly four other guests. The view over the Capitolio was absurdly good. The lobby espresso bar was out of milk for two days straight, and nobody seemed surprised but me.

That contradiction is the whole story of Cuban hospitality in 2026. The state keeps pouring money into glass towers while the guests who'd fill them stay away, and the private sector, the casas particulares and small boutique houses, quietly does the actual work of looking after travelers. My position after a decade of reviewing properties on the island: book private first, book foreign-managed second, and treat the big state all-inclusives as a last resort.

The Government Kept Building While the Guests Stopped Coming

Havana's skyline got a new tallest building in February 2025. Torre K, a 42-story tower on 23rd Street in Vedado, opened as a luxury hotel at a reported cost of around 200 million dollars, in a city where blackouts were rolling through entire neighborhoods that same winter. I walked its block this past January. The lobby gleamed. The street outside was dark.

The numbers behind that image are brutal. Cuba received roughly 2.2 million international visitors in 2024 according to the national statistics office ONEI, down from a peak of about 4.7 million in 2018. Hotel occupancy across the island has hovered near 25 percent, a figure Reuters has reported alongside the government's continued hotel construction spree. Three out of four rooms, empty, most nights.

Here's why you should care as a guest and not just as an economist... an empty luxury hotel is not a luxurious place to stay. Kitchens scale down. Staff get reassigned. The pool bar opens when someone remembers. A five-star rating on a Cuban state property describes the building, not the experience.

Where a Foreign Flag on the Door Actually Matters

There's an exception to my skepticism, and it's management. Cuba's government owns nearly all its hotels, but a handful are operated by foreign chains, Kempinski, Iberostar, Melia, Accor, and the gap between a state-run tower and a foreign-run one is wider in Cuba than anywhere else I review. Same ownership on paper. Completely different breakfast.

The Kempinski Manzana remains the most consistent luxury address in Havana, in my experience, with the SO/ Paseo del Prado close behind for its Malecon-facing rooms. Expect 250 to 450 dollars a night, working air conditioning, and a generator that actually kicks in. But even here, temper your expectations. When I tested room service at two foreign-managed properties in 2024, one delivered in 25 minutes and the other never delivered at all. Nobody called. That's Cuba, at any price point.

And American travelers have an extra layer to check. Many state-affiliated properties sit on the U.S. government's Cuba Restricted List, which makes them off-limits for U.S. citizens to patronize. Check the list before you book, not at check-in.

Casas Particulares Grew Up, and They're the Smart Money

The private guesthouse scene has evolved far past the spare-bedroom-with-a-fan cliche. Havana now has genuine boutique properties, Paseo 206 in Vedado, Malecon 663 with its art-stuffed rooms, and dozens of restored mansions run by owners who treat a bad review as a personal crisis. Rates run 80 to 200 dollars. The owners solve problems the state simply can't: they keep rooftop water tanks filled, they know which paladar has lobster this week, and the good ones run their own generators.

I'll be honest about how I learned this. My friend Leila booked three nights at a casa in Trinidad last spring, and the owner's mother met her at the bus station holding a handwritten cardboard sign, then fed her so much ropa vieja she slept through her walking tour. Meanwhile I once stood in a state hotel lobby trying to pay a bar tab with a U.S.-issued Amex like a complete amateur. Twenty minutes of apologetic Spanish later, a German couple covered my mojito. I'm still not sure the bartender ever expected the card to work. He just enjoyed the show.

So the trade-off is real but small. You give up the rooftop infinity pool and the spa menu. You gain reliability, food cooked to order, and a host with a vested interest in your trip going well. In today's Cuba, that host is the luxury.

The Three Questions to Ask Before You Pay Anyone

Cuba's national grid failed outright in October 2024, and rolling outages have continued since. Water pressure comes and goes. Cards issued by U.S. banks don't work anywhere. None of this should stop you from going, the island rewards travelers who show up prepared, but it does change how you vet a room. Message every property before booking, most respond on WhatsApp within hours, and ask directly:

  • Do you have a generator, and does it cover the rooms or just common areas? Plenty of places power the lobby and let your air conditioning die at 2 a.m.
  • Is there a backup water tank? A cold shower is fine. No shower is a different vacation.
  • What payment do you actually accept? Euros in cash are the safest answer. In most cases, dollars work too, but confirm the rate.

One misconception worth killing: people assume Varadero's big all-inclusives escape these problems because they're tourist zones. Partly true for power, less true for staffing and food variety, and the buffets in 2025 were noticeably thinner than in 2019. At least in my experience, a 90-dollar casa with a full tank beats a 300-dollar tower with excuses.

The bottom line

Before you book anything, send one WhatsApp message today to the property you're considering and ask the generator question and the water question. The speed and honesty of the reply will tell you more about your future stay than any star rating, and if you're American, run the address through the State Department's restricted list while you wait for the answer.

Frequently asked questions

Can Americans legally stay in hotels in Cuba?

Yes, under the Support for the Cuban People travel category, but not in properties on the U.S. State Department's Cuba Restricted List, which includes many state-run hotels. Casas particulares and private guesthouses are the safe choice legally, since staying in them directly supports Cuban families.

Are Cuba's hotels affected by the blackouts?

Yes. The national grid failed in October 2024 and outages have continued since. Foreign-managed hotels and better private casas run generators, but coverage varies, so always ask whether the generator powers guest rooms or only common areas before booking.

Is a casa particular better than a hotel in Cuba?

For most travelers, yes. Private casas and boutique guesthouses cost 80 to 200 dollars, respond fast on WhatsApp, and handle food, water, and power problems better than understaffed state hotels. Book a foreign-managed hotel like the Kempinski only if you specifically want pool-and-spa luxury.
