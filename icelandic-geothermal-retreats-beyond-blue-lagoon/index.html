Iceland's Best Geothermal Retreats Are Nowhere Near the Blue Lagoon

Icelandic geothermal retreats worth the drive: hidden hot spring hotels from Deplar Farm to Kerlingarfjoll, reviewed firsthand beyond the Blue Lagoon.

The best Icelandic geothermal retreats aren't day spas at all. They're hotels with their own hot water, a few hours from Keflavík, where the pool is still quiet at 11 p.m. because the only people in it are guests. In late September 2023 I sat in the steaming pools below Highland Base Kerlingarfjöll with the air at 2°C, four other bathers and a sky going violet over the ridges, and none of it cost me a krona beyond my room rate. Two days earlier I'd shuffled through the Blue Lagoon with what felt like a tour bus and a half, waiting twelve minutes at the bar for a silica mask. My position is simple: book the bath through the bed.

Iceland has more hot water than it knows what to do with. Geothermal heat warms roughly nine in ten homes, according to the National Energy Authority of Iceland, and that abundance is why a farm hotel in the middle of nowhere can keep a pool at 39°C all winter. The crowds, though, are concentrated. The Icelandic Tourist Board counted around 2.2 million foreign visitor departures through Keflavík in 2023, and a huge share of them made the same short transfer to the Reykjanes peninsula. So the smart move is to go where they don't.

The Blue Lagoon Has One Good Argument Left

I'll give it this much. The Retreat at Blue Lagoon is a properly great hotel, and its private lagoon behind the suites is the whole reason, with rooms running upwards of $1,200 a night in high season. But the main lagoon is a theme park with very nice water. Since November 2023 it has also closed several times as eruptions on the Reykjanes peninsula forced evacuations around Grindavík, and two readers have written to me about scrambling to rebook prepaid slots.

Fame protects a place long after it stops earning it. I saw the same pattern when I ranked Amalfi's cliffside hotels and explained why Le Sirenuse lost its crown... the name keeps selling rooms that the experience no longer justifies.

If all you've got is a layover, Sky Lagoon in Kópavogur is the better city soak. The seven-step ritual is theatrical, yes. Its infinity edge faces open ocean instead of a car park, though, and at roughly $90 to $110 it undercuts the Blue Lagoon's premium tier.

Hotels Where the Hot Spring Comes With the Room

This is where your money goes furthest. A guest-only geothermal pool changes the whole trip, because you bathe when the light is good, not when a timed ticket says so.

Deplar Farm, Troll Peninsula

Eleven Experience's converted sheep farm runs north of $2,000 per person per night, all inclusive, with heli-skiing in spring. The indoor-outdoor pool is fed by geothermal water, and I spent forty minutes in it one June midnight watching the sun refuse to set over the Fljót valley. Expensive. Worth it once.

Highland Base Kerlingarfjöll

Opened in 2022 in the interior highlands, it pairs dark timber lodge rooms with bathing pools set in a valley that hisses and steams like a kettle left on. Summer access is by mountain road. Check transfer options before you book outside July and August, since the highland roads close for much of the year.

Hotel Húsafell, West Iceland

The draw here is Canyon Baths, a guided hike down to pools built into a gorge, plus an easy drive to Krauma at Deildartunguhver, Europe's highest-flow hot spring. It's the best value on this list, in most cases under $400 a night.

The North and East Are Where Iceland Bathes Now

Most of the interesting new openings of the last seven years sit far from Reykjavík. When I tested the northern loop in 2024, these were the three I'd send a friend to:

  • GeoSea, Húsavík: seawater heated by boreholes, perched on a cliff above Skjálfandi Bay, with whales passing in summer.
  • Forest Lagoon, Akureyri: opened in 2022 in a stand of birch and pine above Eyjafjörður, about five minutes' drive from town.
  • Vök Baths, near Egilsstaðir: floating pools on Lake Urriðavatn, with a tea bar brewing herbs in hot spring water.

The trade-off is distance. Akureyri is a 45-minute domestic flight or around five hours by car from the capital, and winter driving up north isn't something I'd hand to a first-timer. But if you've got six nights or more, base yourself in the north for two of them and you'll soak in places that still feel local.

Wild Pools, Shower Rules and What Goes Wrong

The big misconception is that wild hot springs are free, public and safe. Many sit on private farmland, temperatures can shift without warning, and bathing at Grjótagjá near Mývatn is banned because the water got dangerously hot after volcanic activity in the 1970s and 80s. Safetravel, run by ICE-SAR, posts current warnings and is worth checking the morning you go. Reykjadalur's hot river is lovely, but it's a 3 km uphill hike each way, and the warm stretch is crowded by noon.

Then there's the shower rule. You wash naked, with soap, before entering any Icelandic pool, and attendants do enforce it. My first time at Laugarvatn Fontana I misread the signs, walked straight into the Konur changing room (that's women), and was steered back out by a very patient grandmother holding a towel. I still think about it. And honestly, I'm not 100% sure the famous silica mud does anything for anyone's skin... my friend Lukas swore it fixed his eczema, then admitted he'd also quit drinking that month, so who knows.

Small studies on geothermal seawater and psoriasis exist, indexed on PubMed, but they're modest. Book these places for the heat, the quiet and the view, not a cure.

The bottom line

Open your Iceland itinerary today and swap one Blue Lagoon slot for a night at a hotel with its own geothermal pool. Before you pay, email the property one question: is the pool fed by your own source, and is it open to non-guests after 8 p.m.? If the answer is yes to the first and no to the second, you've found a retreat, not a day spa with beds.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best alternative to the Blue Lagoon in Iceland?

For a short stay near Reykjavík, Sky Lagoon has better ocean views for less money. With more time, head north to Forest Lagoon or GeoSea, or book a hotel with its own geothermal pool, like Hotel Húsafell or Deplar Farm.

Are Iceland's wild hot springs safe to swim in?

Some are and some aren't. Temperatures can change quickly, many springs sit on private land, and a few, like Grjótagjá, are closed to bathing. Check Safetravel.is the morning you go and never get into water you haven't tested with your hand.

Do you really have to shower naked before Icelandic pools?

Yes. Every pool and lagoon expects you to wash without a swimsuit, using soap, before you go in, and staff will send you back if you skip it. Most changing rooms have a few private stalls if you're shy.
