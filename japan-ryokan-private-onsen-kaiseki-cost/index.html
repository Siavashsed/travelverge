The ¥286,000 Ryokan Night That Taught Me to Pay for the Water

Japan's finest ryokan run $800 to $2,700 a night for two. What private onsen and kaiseki dinners really cost, and where your yen should go.

At Japan's top ryokan, a room with its own open-air bath and a full kaiseki dinner costs most couples ¥120,000 to ¥400,000 a night, roughly $800 to $2,700. The cheaper end is often the better stay. I learned that in March 2024 at a celebrated inn in Hakone. I paid ¥286,000 for two and ate a 13-course dinner I can still recite from memory. Then I found that the cypress tub on my terrace ran on reheated, filtered water that smelled faintly of a hotel pool. Two nights later I was at a 14-room inn in Okuhida, paying less than half that, and soaking in spring water that reached the tub straight from the ground. Spend your money on the water and the kitchen, not the name.

Demand isn't helping. Japan welcomed a record 36.87 million international visitors in 2024, according to the Japan National Tourism Organization, and many of them are chasing the same few dozen famous inns. The number of traditional inns has been falling for decades. Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare counted more than 80,000 ryokan in the early 1980s and fewer than 40,000 by 2017. Japan stayed closed for a long time after the shutdown I covered in what the 2020 border closures meant for independent travelers, and it didn't fully reopen until October 2022. Rates at the top end climbed fast once it did. So it pays to know exactly what you're buying.

How Ryokan Pricing Works, and Why the First Number Misleads You

Almost every ryokan quotes per person, not per room, and that rate usually covers dinner and breakfast, a plan called ippaku nishoku. A listing at ¥85,000 means ¥170,000 for a couple before anything else lands on the bill. I've watched more than one friend book on that misunderstanding and wince at checkout.

The extras add up from there:

  • 10% consumption tax, sometimes included in the quote and sometimes not
  • A 10% to 15% service charge at the grander inns
  • The bathing tax, usually ¥150 per person per night, set under the local tax rules overseen by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications
  • Municipal lodging taxes, which Kyoto City raised in March 2026 to as much as ¥10,000 per person per night on its priciest rooms
  • Drinks, where a beer can run ¥2,000 and a sake pairing far more

My rough rule: double the per-person rate, then add 25%. That's close to your real number. In Hakone, a headline rate of ¥110,000 each came to ¥286,000 once the drinks were in. Tipping is the real grey area. Officially you don't tip. Some older Japanese guests still hand the nakai-san, the room attendant, a kokorozuke envelope with ¥3,000 to ¥5,000 on arrival, and staff at modern inns often hand it back with a smile. I only leave one at traditional family-run places, and I'm still not sure that's the right call.

Private Onsen: The Water Matters More Than the Tub

Free-flowing or recirculated

The misconception I hear most is that a room with its own rotenburo, or open-air bath, guarantees real hot spring water. It doesn't. Japan's Ministry of the Environment administers the Hot Springs Act, which defines onsen water at the source. What happens between the source and your tub is a separate question. The gold standard is gensen kakenagashi, where water flows straight from the spring and spills over the rim. The alternative is junkan: recirculated, filtered, frequently chlorinated, and often reheated.

Neither one is a scam. Recirculation is legal and hygienic, and some springs simply don't produce enough water to feed twenty private tubs. But your skin will notice the difference... and so will your nose. Ask the inn which system feeds your room's bath, because booking pages rarely say.

In-room bath or reservable bath

A kashikiri bath is a private bath you reserve for a slot, usually 40 or 50 minutes, and guests take turns. Most inns charge nothing for it, and it settles the tattoo worry many visitors have about public baths. A room with its own rotenburo can add ¥30,000 to ¥80,000 per person. Is it worth it? For a couple who'll soak four or five times a stay, yes. If you only want one dip before dinner, book the kashikiri and put the savings toward the food.

Kaiseki Dinner Is Where Your Yen Should Go

A proper kaiseki runs nine to fourteen courses built around what's in season that week: matsutake in October, hamo eel in Kansai in July, snow crab on the Sea of Japan coast from November. The cooking tells you more about an inn than the lobby ever will. Across eleven ryokan stays between 2022 and 2025, the inns I'd book again all did the same thing. They served dinner in my room, course by course, brought by the nakai-san who had poured my tea at check-in.

That kind of intimacy comes with rules. Dinner usually starts at 6 or 6:30 and rarely moves, so plan your sightseeing around it. Dietary restrictions are harder than you'd expect. Vegetarian meals are possible with notice. Strict vegan meals often aren't, because dashi made with bonito flakes sits under nearly everything.

Okay, a confession. On my first ryokan stay, years ago in Yamanaka, I tied my yukata right side over left... which, I found out later, is how the dead are dressed for a funeral. The nakai-san didn't say a word. She sort of hummed, retied me like a toddler, bowed, and left. My friend Lena made the same mistake last spring at a different inn and found a little laminated diagram slipped under her door the next morning, which honestly seems like the better system. I'm also not sure anyone missed that I paired a nine-course dinner with three highballs. They noticed. They always notice.

Four Stays, Four Price Points, and Where I'd Book

The famous flagship

Gora Kadan in Hakone, once a summer villa of the imperial family, generally runs ¥100,000 to ¥200,000 per person depending on the room and season, and the private-bath suites cost more. The service is flawless. Even so, you're paying for the history and the gardens as much as for the bath.

The design-led inn

Beniya Mukayu in Yamashiro Onsen, Ishikawa, runs roughly ¥70,000 to ¥130,000 per person. Its spa is genuinely good, and the kitchen cooks food from the Kaga region rather than a generic tour of Japan. For more on this, see The Travel Insurance Claim I Almost Had to Make in Lisbon (And What It Taught Me About Reading the Fine Print).

The small spring-fed inn

The Okuhida inn cost me ¥58,000 per person in 2024, with free-flowing water in every room. The dinner featured Hida beef grilled on a magnolia leaf over charcoal. It had no spa or gift shop, and the rooms were dead quiet.

The Kinosaki compromise

In Kinosaki, good mid-range ryokan charge about ¥35,000 to ¥60,000 per person. Most skip the private bath and give you a pass to the town's seven public bathhouses. Crab season pushes those rates up 30% or more.

My verdict: the best value is ¥50,000 to ¥80,000 per person at a small inn with its own spring. Above that, you're buying architecture and reputation. Those are real things, just not what you came for. I made the same argument about the Maldives betting everything on private-island exclusivity: exclusivity is a product, and you should know when you're paying for it.

The bottom line

Before you book, email the inn you have in mind and ask two questions: is the bath in my room gensen kakenagashi, and what is the total for two people with every tax and service charge included? A good ryokan answers both within a day, in plain numbers. If they dodge either question, book somewhere else.

Frequently asked questions

Is the ryokan price per person or per room?

Almost always per person, and it usually includes dinner and breakfast. Double the quoted rate for a couple, then expect around 25% more for tax, service and drinks.

Does a ryokan room with a private onsen always use real hot spring water?

No. Some in-room baths are gensen kakenagashi, fed straight from the spring, and others recirculate and reheat filtered water. Ask the inn which system your room uses before you book.

How much should I budget for a good ryokan with kaiseki dinner?

For a small, high-quality inn with its own spring, plan on ¥50,000 to ¥80,000 per person per night. Famous flagship ryokan with private-bath suites can pass ¥200,000 per person.
