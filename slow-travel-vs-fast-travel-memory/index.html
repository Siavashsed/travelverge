Slow Travel vs Fast Travel: What You Actually Remember (And What Vanishes)

Fast travel gives you stamps. Slow travel gives you stories. Travel journalist Ana Verge on what memory science and 60 countries taught her.

Words by Ana Verge · June 05, 2026

You already know the answer. You just don't want to admit it. Think back to your last big trip: the one where you hit six cities in nine days. What do you actually remember? A few meals, maybe one conversation, and a blur of airport signage. I did exactly that trip in 2019, a nine-day sprint through Portugal, Spain, and Morocco, and what I can recall with any real texture amounts to maybe four hours of lived experience: the smell of charcoal fish in Cascais on a Tuesday afternoon, an argument in Fez over a taxi fare, and the specific shade of orange the tiles were in a riad courtyard. Everything else has collapsed into a general feeling of having been tired. That's not a travel memory. That's a receipt.

This isn't about being precious or anti-tourist. Fast travel isn't wrong. But there's a real cost to it, and it shows up later in ways most people don't anticipate. The debate between slow travel and fast travel isn't really about pace. It's about what gets encoded in your brain versus what gets discarded. And the science on this is clearer than most travel writers want to admit, because admitting it means questioning their entire publishing model of 'best things to do in 48 hours.'

What Memory Science Actually Says About Novel Experiences

Your brain doesn't record experiences like a camera. It records novelty, emotion, and meaning. When everything is new, which is the case on day one of any trip, your brain expands its sense of time. But after the third cathedral, the fifth market, the eighth flight, the mechanism breaks down. You're no longer processing novelty; you're processing repetition in new clothing. And repetition compresses memory. This is why a week of slow travel in one place can feel richer in retrospect than three weeks of fast travel across a continent.

Research published in Psychological Science found that people systematically underestimate how much they'll remember ordinary moments and overestimate how well they'll remember peak experiences. The implication for travel is direct: the 'peak experiences' you chase by cramming in landmarks are not actually what sticks. What sticks is context, repetition of a specific environment, and emotional weight. A slow trip gives you all three. A fast trip gives you peaks with no surrounding terrain.

There's also the 'holiday paradox,' documented in research by psychologist Claudia Hammond, which describes how busy, event-packed holidays feel short while they're happening but long in retrospect, while slow, uneventful ones feel long in the moment but short in memory. Neither outcome is obviously better. But knowing this changes how you might choose to structure a trip, depending on what you're actually optimizing for.

The Specific Things Fast Travel Deletes From Your Brain

I've seen this pattern across probably thirty of the sixty countries I've visited: when I traveled fast, I remembered the infrastructure more than the place. I remember what the hostels looked like. I remember train schedules and which apps worked offline. I remember logistics. The actual texture of the city, the way light moved through it at different hours, what the locals actually talked about when they weren't talking to tourists... that's almost entirely gone. Fast travel trains you to be an excellent manager of travel, and a poor witness to it.

There's also a documentation trap. A 2013 study in Psychological Science by Linda Henkel at Fairfield University found that photographing objects reduced people's ability to remember them later, what she called a 'photo-taking impairment effect.' Fast travelers photograph everything because they're moving too quickly to absorb anything. The photo becomes the memory, and a photo is not a memory. It's a thumbnail. When I went back through my own photos from a ten-day trip to Japan in 2017, I found hundreds of images I had no emotional connection to whatsoever. Temples I couldn't name. Streets I couldn't place. The things fast travel reliably deletes:

  • Neighborhood rhythms (you never see the same corner twice)
  • Local conversation (you're always a first-time visitor everywhere)
  • Your own interiority (there's no downtime to process what you've seen)
  • The specific sensory texture of a place (because you're always arriving or leaving)

What Slow Travel Actually Gives You That Isn't Romanticized Nonsense

Okay, I need to be honest here, because the slow travel discourse can get insufferable. Not everyone has six weeks to spend in one place. Not everyone wants to. My friend Sarah tried a month-long slow travel stint in Tbilisi in 2022 and honestly found it isolating and a bit dull by week three, and she travels constantly for work. The version of slow travel sold in lifestyle blogs, all sourdough and co-working cafes and sunset wine, is a specific economic and personality profile. I'm not selling that. I'm talking about something more specific: **the relationship between time-in-place and depth of memory formation**, which is a real thing regardless of whether you romanticize it.

What actually happens when you stay somewhere for two weeks instead of two days? You start to recognize faces. You develop preferences between two coffee shops. You have a bad day and have to deal with it locally instead of moving on. You hear the same argument between neighbors three mornings in a row. These are not Instagram moments. They're the raw material of actual memory. I spent three weeks in Oaxaca in 2021 and I can still tell you which side of Calle Macedonio Alcalá gets shade in the afternoon, which vendor at the Benito Juárez market sold the best tlayudas, and what the church bells sounded like at 6am. I was there. I was actually there.

And the logistics of slow travel are easier than fast travel in almost every measurable way. Fewer flights means fewer things that can go wrong with documents and insurance. If you've ever had to untangle a travel insurance claim mid-trip, you know exactly how much a single disruption can derail everything. I wrote about a near-miss travel insurance situation in Lisbon that taught me more about reading the fine print than any advice column ever had. That kind of disruption hits harder when you're moving every two days and the entire itinerary depends on a single connection.

How to Make Either Style of Travel Actually Stick in Memory

Here's my editorial position, plainly: **slow travel produces richer memories in almost all cases**, but the gap between slow and fast travel can be partially closed by how deliberately you engage, rather than just by how long you stay. A distracted person will have shallow memories of three months in one city. An intentional person can extract genuine depth from four days if they're willing to actually be somewhere instead of documenting it.

So the practical advice isn't 'always travel slowly.' It's: **build one anchor experience into every destination**. Not a landmark. An experience that requires you to return. A cooking class where you have to go buy ingredients. A language exchange meetup that happens twice a week. A running route you do every morning. Something that forces repetition, because repetition is what your brain uses to decide something was real and worth keeping. This is why people remember their second day somewhere better than their first: the second day has prior context. The first day is just overwhelming input. If you're a fast traveler by necessity, the single highest-leverage change you can make is to stop photographing everything and start writing one paragraph at the end of each day. Not a diary. One paragraph, in your own words, about something specific that happened. Research from the American Psychological Association consistently links expressive writing to stronger autobiographical memory consolidation. You'll remember more, and what you remember will be yours. Not a gallery of images you took on autopilot while moving too fast to feel anything. And the pandemic years, when travel documentation suddenly required serious attention to things like the EU Digital COVID Certificate and health verification systems, showed us collectively that travel isn't just consumption. It requires engagement with reality. Slow travel asks the same thing of you, just more pleasantly.

Before your next trip, pick one place on your itinerary and add two extra days to it. Not to do more things. To do fewer things twice. Go back to the same market. Eat at the same restaurant two nights in a row. Walk the same street in the morning and at night. You'll feel slightly bored, and that boredom is exactly where real memory forms. Boredom means your brain has stopped being overwhelmed and started actually paying attention.
