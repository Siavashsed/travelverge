Botswana's Fly-In Camps Cost $2,400 a Night. Season Decides If It's Worth It

A firsthand comparison of luxury safari lodges in Botswana across green, shoulder and dry season, with real rates and honest trade-offs.

The single biggest factor in whether a Botswana fly-in camp feels like the best money you've ever spent or a quietly expensive mistake isn't the camp. It's the month you booked. I learned that properly in February 2023, sitting in a canvas tent at a Linyanti concession while rain hammered the roof for nine straight hours and our morning drive got cancelled, and I remember thinking about the $1,150 nightly rate with a kind of numb amusement. Then the sky cleared at 4pm, we drove out, and I watched a leopard drag an impala up a leadwood tree in light so gold it looked staged. Both things were true. That's the whole argument of this piece: Botswana's seasons don't make camps better or worse, they make them completely different products sold under the same name.

Most people book July or August because a travel agent told them that's peak game viewing, which is correct and also means you're paying roughly double for a camp that's full, a delta that's flooded, and sightings shared with three other vehicles at the same waterhole. I've now stayed in Botswana camps in five different months across four trips, and my honest position is that the shoulder months get unfairly ignored by people who could afford better trips for less if they just moved their dates six weeks.

What You Actually Pay, Month by Month

Let's get the money straight, because camp websites are cagey about it. High season at the top tier of Botswana camps, think Mombo, Jao, Duba Plains, Zarafa, runs somewhere between $1,900 and $3,200 per person per night all-inclusive. Green season at those same properties drops to roughly $850 to $1,400. That's not a small discount. On a seven-night trip for two, you're talking about a swing of $15,000 or more for identical beds, identical chefs, identical guides.

Botswana built this on purpose. The country's high-value, low-volume tourism policy caps beds inside concessions, which is why you'll go three hours on a drive without seeing another vehicle. Tourism contributes a meaningful share of the economy here, and Botswana's own government reporting through Statistics Botswana tracks tourism as one of the largest non-mining contributors to GDP. The scarcity is policy, not marketing.

And here's the thing... the price gap doesn't map cleanly onto experience quality. It maps onto water levels and grass height. Those two variables drive almost everything you'll care about.

Dry Season, June Through October, and Why It Deserves the Hype

From roughly late May, the Okavango floodwaters that fell as rain in the Angolan highlands months earlier arrive in the delta. It's a genuinely strange thing to sit with: the delta floods during Botswana's dry season, fed by rain that fell 1,000 kilometres away. Grass is short and brown, ephemeral pans have dried up, and animals concentrate hard around permanent water.

Predator viewing peaks. In September 2024 at a Khwai-area camp I had four separate wild dog sightings in five days, one of them a hunt at close range that ended about forty metres from the vehicle. That density is real and I won't pretend otherwise.

The trade-off nobody puts in the brochure: August and September are also when camps run at capacity, when the good guides are stretched thin, and when your "private" concession has more traffic than you were promised. Air Botswana and the light-aircraft operators are booked solid, so your transfer windows get rigid. Dust is constant. And the photography, honestly, is flat. Bleached skies, brown grass, harsh midday light from about 9am.

  • Best for: first-time safari, guaranteed volume of sightings, predator behaviour
  • Worst for: photographers chasing texture and colour, anyone allergic to other vehicles
  • Book: 12 to 14 months ahead for the top camps, no exaggeration

Green Season, December Through March, Is the Connoisseur's Play

What changes

Rain arrives, the Kalahari transforms, and the whole colour palette shifts to something almost absurd. Skies stack up with cloud. Zebra migrate into the Makgadikgadi and Nxai Pan in numbers that National Geographic has covered as one of Africa's longest recorded land mammal migrations, a round trip of around 500 kilometres documented through GPS collaring work published in 2012. Newborn impala and wildebeest are everywhere, which means predators are active and visible in a different way.

Birding goes from good to genuinely world-class as migrants arrive. Carmine bee-eaters, woodland kingfishers, the lot.

What you sacrifice

Grass gets tall. Sightings get harder. Some camps in the Linyanti and parts of the delta simply close for a few weeks because the roads become impassable. Your morning drive can and will get cancelled by weather, as mine did.

But you're paying 45 to 55 percent less, the camp is a third full, and the guide has time to actually teach you something. In my experience the guiding quality you receive in February is higher than in August purely because nobody is rushed.

The Shoulder Months Are the Answer Most People Miss

April, May and November. That's my genuine recommendation and I'll argue for it against anyone. April and May give you receding grass, rising floodwaters, mild temperatures, and rates that sit maybe 20 to 30 percent under peak. November gives you the first storms, dramatic light, and camps that are half empty before the December holiday rush.

I brought a friend on his first safari in May 2022 and he spent the first two days convinced I'd sold him a bad month because we weren't tripping over lions. Then on day three we sat with a pack of nineteen wild dogs for maybe forty minutes with nobody else around, and he went quiet in a way I've only seen a few times. He still texts me about it. I'm not going to claim May always delivers that, because it doesn't, and there were mornings on that trip where the best thing we saw was a very committed dung beetle. But the ratio of good-to-great days across shoulder months has been better for me than peak, and I've stopped apologising for saying so.

One misconception worth killing: people assume mosquitoes and malaria risk make green and shoulder season dangerous. Risk is real and prophylaxis matters, but the CDC classifies malaria transmission in northern Botswana as seasonal and concentrated in the November to June window, and every reputable camp provides nets, repellent and treated tents as standard. It's a managed risk, not a reason to write off half the calendar.

The bottom line

Do this today: pull up the rate sheet for whichever camp you've been dreaming about, find the exact same room in May and in August, and put the two numbers side by side. If the gap is more than about $700 a night, take the shoulder date and spend the difference on an extra three nights or a second camp in a different ecosystem. Two contrasting concessions in the shoulder season will beat one famous camp in August almost every time.

Frequently asked questions

When is the cheapest time to stay at a Botswana fly-in camp?

January through March, the green season, where top-tier camps drop to roughly $850 to $1,400 per person per night versus $1,900 to $3,200 in July and August. Some camps close for a few weeks during this window, so confirm operating dates before you book flights.

How far in advance do I need to book a Botswana safari camp?

For July through October at the well-known camps, 12 to 14 months. Shoulder and green season you can often find space 4 to 6 months out, and occasionally less if you're flexible on which concession you land in.

Is green season game viewing actually bad in Botswana?

It's harder, not bad. Tall grass and dispersed water mean fewer guaranteed sightings, but you get newborn animals, migrating zebra in the Makgadikgadi, exceptional birding and far fewer vehicles. Predator sightings take more patience and a better guide.
