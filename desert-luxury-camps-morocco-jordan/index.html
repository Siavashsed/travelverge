Desert Luxury Camps in Morocco and Jordan Start Where the Road Ends

Desert luxury camps in Morocco and Jordan: why remoteness beats thread count, which deserts to pick, and when the splurge pays off.

You can sleep in real luxury in the Moroccan Sahara or in Jordan's Wadi Rum, but the comfort you're paying for is rarely the bed. What you're really buying is distance from the next camp, and that's where most travelers get it wrong. In February 2024 I paid $310 for a transparent dome outside Rum village. At 2 a.m. I could hear a generator, a Bluetooth speaker playing Dua Lipa, and a man three tents over arguing about breakfast times. Four nights later, in a canvas tent at Erg Chigaga with a copper tub filled by bucket and no Wi-Fi at all, I paid less and slept better than I have in most five-star hotels. Pay for remoteness first. Everything else is a bonus.

Wadi Rum has been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2011. That status protects the sandstone, but it doesn't protect the skyline from the camp boom along the protected area's edge. And the sky is the whole point. A 2016 atlas published in Science Advances found that more than 80% of the world's population lives under light-polluted skies, and the Milky Way is hidden from over a third of humanity. You're flying a long way to see something most people never get to see. A ring of floodlit domes a few hundred meters away gives a lot of that back.

Wadi Rum Rewards You for Driving Past the Dome Clusters

Most of the photogenic bubble tents you've seen online sit in tight clusters a short drive from the visitor center. They're comfortable, and the bathrooms are better than you'd expect. But you're sharing the night with dozens of other guests, a few strings of fairy lights and, often, the glow of the village itself.

The greenhouse problem

Domes look wonderful at sunset and miserable at 3 p.m. In October I checked the little thermometer on my nightstand mid-afternoon and it read 34°C (93°F), with the air conditioner running flat out. If you're booking a dome between April and October, plan to be out of it all afternoon. Canvas Bedouin-style tents with thick goat-hair walls stay cooler and, in my opinion, feel more like the place.

Here's the question I now ask every camp before I book: how long is the 4x4 transfer from the visitor center? Under 15 minutes usually means neighbors. Forty minutes or more, over sand, usually means silence.

Morocco Is Three Different Deserts, and Only Two Have Dunes

Travelers lump Morocco's camps together, which is a mistake. These are very different nights:

  • Agafay: about 40 minutes from Marrakech, rocky and lunar, no dunes. Camps run roughly $250 to $600 a night.
  • Erg Chebbi near Merzouga: proper dunes, some around 150 meters high, reached by paved road. Expect lots of camps and quad bikes on weekends.
  • Erg Chigaga: around 60 kilometers of rough track past M'Hamid, about two hours by 4x4. It's the quiet one, and it's where I'd send you.

Agafay gets a lot of criticism from purists, and some of it is deserved. It makes a gorgeous dinner and a pleasant night if you only have a weekend. But it isn't the Sahara, and the lights of Marrakech sit low on the horizon. If dunes are the dream, pay for the extra drive.

My own mistake was booking Erg Chebbi on a Saturday during a Moroccan school holiday. The camp was lovely. The engines on the ridge behind it until sunset were not.

What Luxury Means When the Nearest Water Main Is Hours Away

Every shower at a deep-desert camp was trucked in, and every hot one was heated by solar, gas or a staff member with a kettle. Good camps hide that work. Great ones tell you about it, and that tells you the operation is honest. Ask whether the toilets flush, whether hot water runs at night, and how the power works. A camp that answers vaguely about water will be vague about other things too.

The biggest misconception is temperature. Deserts aren't warm at night. In January I've woken to near-freezing air in both Wadi Rum and Erg Chigaga, and the best camps bring hot water bottles and wood stoves without being asked.

My friend Johanna tried the "pack light, it's the desert" approach in Erg Chebbi one January and honestly... she spent dinner wrapped in a carpet borrowed from the dining tent. I laughed at her until about 1 a.m., when I realized my own "thermal layer" was a cotton hoodie I'd bought at Frankfurt airport. The staff brought one hot water bottle to our tent. I'm still not sure who it was meant for. We split it, badly.

Season Decides Whether the Splurge Pays Off

March to April and October to November are the sweet spots in both countries: warm days, cold but bearable nights, and clear skies. Summer afternoons regularly pass 40°C (104°F), and no amount of thread count fixes that. It's the same logic that decides whether Botswana's $2,400-a-night fly-in camps are worth the rate in a given season. The setting is the product, so you buy the setting at its best.

So be wary of new camps that lead with architecture. A shiny build is no guarantee anyone actually wants to stay there. Cuba proved that at scale with a 42-story luxury tower that almost nobody sleeps in. In the desert, the camps I return to are the ones run by families who've worked the same valley for decades, at least in my experience. They know which dune blocks the wind.

Moon phase matters more than people think, too. A full moon is beautiful, but it washes out the stars. Book within a few days of the new moon if the sky is your reason for going, and check DarkSky International for viewing guidance before you go.

The bottom line

Before you pay a deposit, open the camp's location in satellite view on Google Maps and count the white roofs within two kilometers. If you can count more than five, keep looking.

Frequently asked questions

Is Wadi Rum or the Moroccan Sahara better for a luxury desert camp?

Wadi Rum is easier to reach and pairs well with Petra, and its red sandstone is unlike anything in Morocco. Morocco's Erg Chigaga has bigger dunes and more isolation, but it takes a long drive. Pick Jordan if you're short on time and Morocco if you want real dunes and silence.

How cold does it get at night in desert camps in winter?

From December to February, nights in both Wadi Rum and the Moroccan Sahara can drop close to freezing. Pack real thermal layers and a warm hat, and ask the camp whether tents have heating or wood stoves.

Are the bubble tents in Wadi Rum worth the price?

They're comfortable and great for stargazing from bed, but most sit in busy clusters near the village and get very hot in the afternoon. They're worth it in cooler months if you pick one far from the main clusters. In summer, a traditional canvas tent is the better choice.
