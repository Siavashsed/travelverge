All-Inclusive Resorts Are Worth It, But Only If You Choose Small

Are all-inclusive resorts worth the money? A resort reviewer explains what the wristband really buys, where it fails, and how to pick well.

Yes, an all-inclusive resort can be worth it, but the price on the booking page tells you almost nothing about whether yours will be. Size, restaurant count and how many guests share the pool decide it far more than the nightly rate does.

I learned that at 7:40 on a March morning in Cancun, standing in a buffet line of about 60 people for an omelet. My room had cost $412 a night per couple. The omelet station had one cook. By the time mine arrived, my coffee had gone cold and my husband had given up and eaten a croissant standing by the juice dispenser.

I've reviewed resorts and spas for years, and I've slept in roughly forty all-inclusives across Mexico, Jamaica, the Dominican Republic and the Maldives. The format isn't the problem. The problem is that the wristband rewards volume, and volume is the enemy of anything you'd call luxury. So here's what I look for now, and what I'd tell a friend before she hands over a deposit.

What the Wristband Really Buys You

Start with the honest math. At a mid-market property, the daily rate often lands somewhere between $250 and $450 per person, and a decent dinner with two cocktails at a comparable non-inclusive resort would cost you $90 to $130 anyway. If you drink, eat three meals and book a snorkel trip, the package usually pays for itself by day two. If you're a light eater who skips alcohol, it frequently doesn't.

There's a second benefit people undersell: predictability. You stop doing arithmetic. You stop eyeing the wine list. I've watched guests relax visibly on the second afternoon, once the part of the brain that tallies costs finally quits. That calm is a real product, and it's the best argument for the format.

But the same wristband creates a quiet incentive problem. The resort already has your money, so it makes its margin by controlling what you consume. Think pre-poured wine, house spirits behind the bar, and the good restaurant requiring a reservation made at 6 a.m.

Why Scale Decides Everything

The pattern I've seen repeatedly is simple. Properties with 400-plus rooms run food like a stadium. Properties under about 60 rooms run it like a kitchen. I made the full argument in why the best luxury beach resorts have fewer than 60 rooms, and it applies doubly to all-inclusives, where every guest eats every meal on site.

Here's the quick scan I run before booking:

  • Room count: under 100 is a good sign, under 60 is better.
  • A la carte dining: at least three sit-down restaurants without a booking scramble.
  • Top-shelf included: named spirits, not just "premium brands".
  • Spa and excursions: at least one treatment or guided outing in the rate.

The Tipping and Add-On Trap

Read the exclusions line. Some resorts bill the "all" down to a rubber stamp, then charge extra for the specialty restaurant, the motorized water sports, the minibar and the airport transfer. I once added up $186 of surprises on a four-night stay that had been sold to me as fully covered.

Where the Model Quietly Fails

Let me be fair, because there's real debate here. Critics of the format point to economic leakage, the share of your spending that never reaches local people. The UN Environment Programme has long cited estimates that a large share of tourist spending in package-style tourism flows back to foreign-owned firms, though the exact figure varies by destination and is contested. Smaller, locally owned all-inclusives tend to do better, which is one more reason to go small.

Then there's your stomach. The CDC Yellow Book says travelers' diarrhea affects roughly 30 to 70 percent of travelers, depending on destination and season. Buffets held at warm temperatures for hours are a classic culprit, and I've lost two beach days to it myself.

I'm not 100% sure this works for everyone, but here's my embarrassing confession. On my third trip I tried to "beat" the system by eating five times a day. My friend Dana, who'd come along, still reminds me I fell asleep face-down in a lounger holding an empty piña colada glass and woke up with a perfect sunburnt rectangle on my back. The wristband makes you stupid. Plan for that.

How to Book One You Won't Regret

Go in shoulder season. According to Statista, the all-inclusive segment has kept growing in the Caribbean in recent years, which means peak weeks are crowded and priced to match. In May or late October, I've paid 30 to 40 percent less for the same room and watched staff actually have time to chat.

Email the property before you book. Ask three plain questions: how many guests are in-house that week, which restaurants need reservations, and what the policy is on spirits. A confident, specific reply is a great sign... a vague one tells you what the kitchen looks like.

And one more thing. Choose the property that fits your habits, not the one with the longest amenity list. A quiet couple who wants a spa and a book will be happier at a 40-room hideaway than at a resort with six pools and a foam party.

The bottom line

Before you book anything today, find the resort's room count and count its sit-down restaurants. If the rooms outnumber the restaurants by more than 25 to 1, keep looking.

Frequently asked questions

Are all-inclusive resorts worth the money?

They usually are if you drink alcohol, eat three meals on site and book at least one activity. If you eat lightly and skip drinks, paying a la carte at a comparable resort often costs less.

What's the best time of year to book an all-inclusive?

Shoulder season, such as May or late October in the Caribbean, typically saves 30 to 40 percent. You also get shorter lines and a more attentive staff.

Should I tip at an all-inclusive resort?

Yes, in most cases. Staff wages at many properties depend partly on tips, so carrying small bills for bartenders, housekeeping and servers is a good idea even when the brochure says gratuities are included.
